# Session (2026-11-04T23:48:24.000Z)

**Title:** the mini-scheduler's conditional claim - the timeout's root cause, a database lab, `_origin` and the worked examples

## Context

Eric: the field's state-resolver timeouts were not a cold start. The resolver held a database record lock so that only
one of the two scheduler pods would write, and the scheduler is designed for an optimistic write. The development team
was asked to remove the lock. Would the original design (a random 0-9 s delay, then a read and a write) still pick one
pod when both pick the same second? Docker (colima) was started so this session could test against a real database.

## Findings (the code)

- `AssignJob` defers by WHOLE seconds, `crypto.nextInt(0, 1000) % 10`, so two pods share a second 1 time in 10.
- `expires` is a question and `start` a separate message sent after a `true`. A resolver that reads in `expires` and
  writes in `start`, as the sample does, lets two pods that ask within moments of each other both run the job.
- A record lock prevents that, but the losing pod waits on the lock, and the scheduler's timeout records the wait.

## The lab (outside the repository, in the session scratchpad)

MongoDB 8.0 in Docker (`sched-mongo-lab`, left running), the embedded PostgreSQL 18.4 that `reactive-postgres`'s tests
use (cached, no download), the MongoDB Java driver 5.8.1 from Maven Central (Boot 4.1.2's managed version), and a lab
application of two JVMs on the released 4.12.28 mini-scheduler.

- **Direct races (threads at a barrier, 1000 rounds):** read-then-write ran twice in every round (2 and 8 pods, a first
  run and after an expired run); the conditional claim ran exactly once in every round.
- **Two pods of the scheduler:** read-then-write ran twice in 10 of 11 simultaneous firings and 12 of 240 with
  `deferred.start` (5%); the claim ran exactly once in 11 of 11 and 240 of 240.
- **Eric's question on the index:** with the job name in its own field, no index and a plain index both let both pods
  insert (2000 documents for 1000 jobs, every round twice); a unique index gave one winner every round.
- **Eric's proposal, write - wait 1 s - read back and compare `_origin`:** one winner in 60 of 60 rounds with normal
  timing; with a 1.5 s pause between one pod's read and its write, both ran in 20 of 20. It is Fischer's timed mutual
  exclusion: safe only while every write lands within the wait. Separate MongoDB clients per pod still gave one winner
  in 60 of 60, so the driver keeps no cache of its own writes (Eric's concern); an ORM session, a snapshot transaction
  or a read replica would. Eric then chose the conditional claim and kept `_origin`.
- **The worked examples, run verbatim:** PostgreSQL (`INSERT ... ON CONFLICT (name) DO UPDATE ... WHERE start_time <
  now() - 30 s OR parameters ->> '_origin' matches`) and MongoDB (`updateOne` upsert filtered on the window or
  `parameters._origin`, the duplicate key as the refusal): exactly one winner in 300 of 300 rounds with 2 and with 8
  pods, on a first run and after an expired run, on each database. Negative control: the PostgreSQL upsert without its
  `WHERE` let both pods run in 600 of 600, so ACID alone picks no winner. Lifecycle on both: claim true, retry by the
  same origin true, a peer within the window false, a peer after the window true, the record naming the holder.
- **The retry with `_origin` (this branch, two JVMs):** each pod's first question of every firing answered after 4 s;
  the claim with the `_origin` clause ran exactly once in 10 of 10 firings (4 on one pod, 6 on the other, each loser
  skipping with the new warning); without the clause, 0 of 10. The state record named the holder by the origin that the
  instance's startup line prints. A first rerun of that scenario was invalid - the lab resolver keyed its slow first
  answer on the id, which no longer changes per firing - and was keyed on the firing's time slot and run again.

## What was done (branch `feature/scheduler-conditional-claim`)

- `ScheduledJob.ORIGIN = "_origin"` replaces `FIRING`; `JobExecutor` puts the instance's origin id in the parameters of
  `expires` and `start`, the correlation id goes back to a per-run id, and the warning for "not expired" after a
  timed-out attempt names a peer's claim as a cause. `JobLoader` logs `Scheduler instance <origin> - the state resolver
  receives it as _origin` at startup.
- The mini-scheduler README's *Running more than one instance* replaces *Simple clustering*: the claim, the three
  things it needs (one conditional write, a unique key on the name, the window's bounds), `deferred.start` as load
  spreading only, and the two worked examples; *Slow answers: retries* and the *Time zone* window paragraph follow.
  The example README, both cron files, both sample `StateResolver` contracts and the configuration reference's
  `deferred.start` follow; `ResolverRetryTest` and `ScheduleAdminTest` assert `_origin`. CHANGELOG Unreleased: Changed 2
  and Documentation 1.
